THE STORE IS YOURS
Trainer's guide to running a live session

A plain-English handbook for the facilitator running "The Store Is Yours", the
live retail leadership simulation set in Plumfield Stores. Read it once before
your first session. Keep it beside you when you run one. Everything here matches
the current build of the game.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHAT THIS IS, IN ONE BREATH
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Each team runs a Plumfield store for eight shifts. Every shift they read what is
happening on the floor and make a set of time-boxed leadership decisions on a
shared laptop. Those decisions move live store numbers, and quietly move four
hidden drivers underneath (trust, capability, safety risk and leadership
consistency). It is head-to-head: several teams play the same shifts at the same
time, and a leaderboard tracks them.

You are not a referee and you are not a lecturer. You open the room, brief them,
start each shift, stay out of the way while they play, and then run the debrief
where the real learning happens. The game hands you a tailored question for each
team after every shift, so you always have somewhere to start.

The whole point is self-awareness. There are no right answers, only revealing
ones. Say that early and mean it.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. BEFORE YOU START (KIT AND ROOM)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

You need:

- One screen for you, the facilitator. A laptop is fine, but a big screen or
  projector the whole room can see makes the briefing and debrief much stronger.
- One shared laptop per team. Teams gather round it and decide together. Sharing
  the laptop is deliberate; how they make decisions as a group is part of what
  you are watching.
- A steady internet connection for every device. The game is live and talks to a
  server the whole time. Weak wifi shows up as teams dropping and rejoining.
- Between two and eight teams. Four is the sweet spot. More teams means a busier
  debrief but a richer room.

A word of warning worth taking seriously: the game holds everything in memory on
the server. If the server restarts mid-session, every live session is wiped and
you cannot get it back. Do not let anyone deploy an update while you are running.
Run your session start to finish on a stable build.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. SETTING THE GAME UP
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

There are two roles, and each has its own screen. You are the Facilitator. The
teams are Teams. They never share a screen with you.

To create the session (you):

1. Open the game's web address. You land on the splash screen. Press "Launch
   simulation". The game goes full screen.
2. Choose the Facilitator card on the left.
3. Set the number of teams with the plus and minus buttons (two to eight). You
   can change your mind later by just starting with fewer; you do not have to
   wait for a full house.
4. Press "Create session". You are taken to your Facilitator dashboard, and a
   five-character session code appears top right (something like A7K2N).

Keep that dashboard open and do not lose the link. Your access is tied to a
private token in that web address. If you close it, reopening from the same
browser will usually get you back in; if you open a fresh browser with just the
plain address, it will refuse you. Treat the session as yours alone.

To get the teams in (them):

1. Each team opens the same web address on their laptop and presses "Launch
   simulation".
2. They choose the Team card on the right, then "Join a session".
3. They type your five-character code and a team name (for example "North Store"),
   and press "Join session".

As each team joins, you see them appear on your dashboard in the lobby, filling
the slots you set. When everyone is in, you are ready to brief. You can start
before every slot is filled if a team is running late.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. HOW A SESSION RUNS, START TO FINISH
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The session moves through a fixed set of phases. You drive the moves between
them. Only the shifts themselves are on a hard clock; everything else waits for
you, so you can never be rushed through a briefing or a debrief.

  Lobby        Teams join. You wait until you are ready.
  Briefing     You walk the room through the screen, at your own pace.
  Shift 1      Five minutes on the clock. Teams decide.
  Debrief 1    You debrief that shift. Takes as long as you let it.
  Shift 2 ...  And so on, for eight shifts in total.
  Debrief 8    The last shift debrief.
  Full debrief One reflective conversation across the whole session.
  Close        You close the session and can download a report.

Rough timings: a shift is five minutes of play. A quick between-shift debrief is
five to ten minutes. Budget a good half hour for the briefing plus first shift,
then settle into a rhythm. A full run of eight shifts with proper debriefs is a
half-day workshop, not a lunch-hour filler. You control the pace throughout.

The one clock: a shift lasts five minutes. It ends when the timer hits zero, or
sooner if every team has submitted. You can also end it early yourself.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. HOW THE GAME WORKS (SO YOU CAN EXPLAIN IT)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The store, in numbers. Every store is measured against five goals: Sales,
Colleagues, Service, Costs and Risk. Underneath those five goals sit ten specific
measures (things like sales against budget, availability, colleague engagement,
customer satisfaction, labour and waste control, audits). The top-of-screen HUD
shows the five goals at a glance; teams can open a full "All 10" view to see
every measure with its real value against baseline and target. Each team also
starts with sixteen weeks of history behind them, so even the first shift has a
trend to read into.

The decisions. Each shift, a team makes seven decisions grouped into five tabs:

  Focus       A priority to lead with, and how they will act on it. Both required.
  Team        A leadership style, and how they split their people's time across
              four areas of the store (the split must add up to 100 percent).
              Both required.
  Issue       Optionally, they point their effort at one of the three live issues,
              or deliberately stay broad. Either is a real choice.
  People      When a named member of their team needs them this shift, how they
              respond. Required whenever that person appears.
  Confidence  How hard they back the whole plan: Cautious, Measured or Confident.
              Required.

Confidence is the sting. It multiplies everything that happens that shift, good
and bad. Cautious dampens the swing (about three-quarters). Measured is straight.
Confident amplifies it (about a third more either way). Backing a good plan hard
pays off; backing a poor one hard hurts more.

The hidden drivers. Under the visible numbers sit four things the game never
shows during a shift: trust, capability, safety risk and leadership consistency.
They shape the score without appearing on the board. They are revealed after a
shift resolves, on the team's own results screen and on your coaching cards. They
are where a lot of the leadership learning lives.

What teams read before they act. On the left of their screen is Context: exactly
three live Issues (what is happening in the store right now) and up to two Alerts
from head office and operations. They should read these before deciding.

The handover, and why it lies. Every team can open a "Handover" document, a note
from the outgoing manager, Ray Dolan. It is written to sound confident and
helpful and it is deliberately unreliable. Ray tempts teams to act early on his
word; teams who take him at face value make confident wrong moves, and teams who
read him against what they actually see on the floor do better. You have a full
answer key for this (see the debrief section). It is one of the richest threads
in the whole game.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. THE RANDOM EVENTS, AND THE ODDS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The one source of genuine randomness you need to understand is disruptions. A
disruption is something going wrong mid-shift: a fire alarm, a walkout, a till
outage, a viral complaint, and so on.

The odds, plainly:

- Every shift has a fifty-fifty chance of a disruption. It is decided fresh each
  shift, so some shifts break and some stay completely clean.
- When one does fire, it lands somewhere in the first half of the shift (the
  first two and a half minutes), at a moment nobody can predict.
- It hits every team at the same instant. That keeps it fair: no team gets an
  easier ride than another. It is the same store weather for everyone.
- You cannot trigger or cancel one. There is deliberately no "disrupt now" button.
  The unpredictability is the teaching point.

What that means across a full session of eight shifts: expect roughly four
disruptions on average, but any given run can have more or fewer. It is possible,
though very unlikely, to get a clean sweep or a run where nearly every shift
breaks. Do not promise the room a disruption, and do not act surprised by a quiet
shift; a shift with nothing going wrong tempts teams into their own revealing
choices, which is useful in itself.

The other "events" are not random in the same way but are worth knowing:

- Issues: there are always exactly three live issues per shift.
- Alerts: up to two head-office or operational alerts, plus one more if a
  disruption is running, so never more than three messages at once.
- People moments: on some shifts a named direct report comes to the manager with
  a situation. When that happens, the People tab becomes required. How teams
  respond quietly moves trust and capability.

On your own screen, when a disruption is live during a shift, a clear "Disruption
live" marker shows up in your control strip, so you always know the room is in
the thick of one.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. CONTROLLING THE GAME (YOUR DASHBOARD)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Your Facilitator dashboard is built so the things you actually touch sit at the
top, and the things you read sit below. From the top down:

- The header. Your session code (share this with latecomers), the shift clock,
  and how many teams are in. A full-screen toggle sits here too.

- The guidance banner. A short prompt telling you what this phase is for, with
  the single most important button on the right. That button always does the
  next big thing: "Start briefing", then "Start Shift 1", then "Start Shift 2"
  after each debrief, and finally "Begin debrief" and "Close session". If you
  only remember one control, remember that this banner button moves you forward.

- The control strip. Directly beneath the banner. This is your moment-to-moment
  control and it changes with the phase:
    During the briefing, it holds the Back and Next buttons that step the whole
    room through the walkthrough, with the step number and the current step's
    talking points.
    During a shift, it holds "End shift early" and a live count of how many teams
    have submitted, plus the "Disruption live" marker when one is running.
    After the session, it holds the "Download report" button.
  On the right of this strip you always see how many teams are in.

- The leaderboard. A compact ranking down the side, so you can see who is ahead
  and who is moving, without it dominating the screen.

- The coaching cards. The large area of the screen. One card per team. This is
  your debrief engine, described in the next section.

How a shift actually ends. It ends on its own when the five minutes run out, or
the moment all teams have submitted, whichever comes first. If the room is clearly
done and you do not want to wait for the clock, press "End shift early". After a
shift ends you move into that shift's results, and the banner button becomes
"Start Shift" for the next one when you are ready.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. RUNNING THE BRIEFING
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The briefing is not a slide deck. It is a guided, animated tour of the real shift
screen, and it happens on every team's laptop at once, in lockstep with you. You
press Next; every team's screen advances to the same step and plays the matching
little demonstration. You talk over it.

To run it: from the lobby, press "Start briefing" in the banner. Then use Back
and Next in the control strip to move through the walkthrough. The strip shows
you the step's title and a short talking script for each one, so you always know
what to say. When you reach the end, press "Start Shift 1" in the banner.

There are eleven steps, and they lead the room through the screen in a natural
order:

  1. Welcome. The whole screen, at rest. Set the scene.
  2. Read your store. The top HUD lights up: five goals, health bars, trends.
  3. See all ten measures. The full ten-metric view opens over the screen, so the
     room sees the real numbers behind the five goals. This is the moment to point
     out baselines and targets. It opens on every team's laptop together and they
     cannot close it; you move everyone on with Next.
  4. What's happening. The Context column: the three issues and the alerts.
  5. Set your focus. The Focus tab: a priority and an action.
  6. Lead your team. The Team tab: leadership style and the people split.
  7. Target an issue. The Issue tab: point your effort, or stay broad.
  8. Handle the people moment. The People tab: a real team member needs you.
  9. Back your plan. The Confidence tab: cautious, measured or confident.
  10. Expect the unexpected. A disruption strikes, to show what one looks like.
  11. The store is yours. The full screen, bright and ready to play.

Keep it moving but do not rush. The briefing is where you set the tone: no right
answers, decisions lock when the clock hits zero, and the numbers usually mean
something even when the handover tells them otherwise. If your venue has the
outgoing-manager welcome video, play it before you start the walkthrough; it
points teams to the handover document.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. FACILITATING THE SHIFT DEBRIEFS (THE PROMPT CARDS)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the heart of your job. After each shift, the game turns your dashboard
into a set of prompt cards, one per team, and hands you tailored questions. You
do not have to invent anything on the spot.

What is on each team's card:

- Who they are, their score and where they rank.
- Their five goals with the direction each one moved this shift.
- Once a shift has resolved, their hidden drivers (trust, capability, safety risk,
  leadership consistency), so you can see what was moving underneath.
- At the top of the card, an "Ask them" question. This is the important part. It
  is written for that specific team, anchored on the single measure that moved
  most for them this shift (or, if they held steady, the thing that barely moved).
  Two teams will get different questions even if they look similar, because the
  question follows their own story.

How to use the cards well:

- Lead with the card's question. Read the "Ask them" line to the team as it is,
  then stop talking. Sit in the silence. The second answer people give is usually
  the honest one.
- Follow up rather than move on. "Say more about that." "What makes you say that?"
  A single question opened up beats six questions rattled through.
- Pick two or three teams, or two or three threads, per shift. You do not have to
  visit every card every shift. Spread the attention across the session.
- Do not read the score out as a verdict. The numbers are a way into the thinking,
  not the point of it. Resist ranking teams against each other out loud; surface
  the difference in approach instead.
- Watch who says "we" and who says "I", and whether teams credit their choices or
  blame the game. Notice where the room goes quiet. That is usually where the
  learning is.

Your guidance banner also gives you a short, room-wide talk track for each shift,
picking up on patterns it has spotted across all teams (for example, everyone
chose the same priority, or the whole room played it cautious). Use those to open
a whole-room conversation, then drop into individual cards.

Going deeper with the printed question bank. Alongside the on-screen questions you
have a fuller bank of open questions, grouped by theme, in the file
"debrief-questions.md". Threads include priorities and focus, acting versus
observing, leadership style, resource trade-offs, the people moment, confidence
and risk, disruptions, reading the store versus trusting the gut, the handover,
team dynamics, emotion and pressure, patterns over time, and transfer back to the
real workplace. Keep it open on your own screen or printed beside you. Pick a
couple that fit what you just watched. The same rule applies: two or three, not
ten, then sit in the silence.

The handover thread (the unreliable narrator). One of the strongest debrief
seams is Ray Dolan's handover. You have a full answer key in
"handover-facilitator-crib.md": it lists, person by person and claim by claim,
where Ray steers teams wrong and what the floor actually shows. Do not pre-empt
it; let teams discover the mismatches themselves across the shifts. Then in the
debrief, ask the open handover questions first ("How much did you trust the
handover?", "Where did what you found differ from what you were told?"), and only
then, where it sharpens the point, name a specific trap from the crib. Frame it as
a leadership lesson in reading people and data for yourself, never as a gotcha.
Ray's sign-off, "trust your gut over the dashboards", is the whole mechanic in one
line; it is worth quoting back to the room and asking whose gut they trusted, his
or their own, and what it cost or saved them.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
10. THE FULL-SESSION DEBRIEF
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

After the eighth shift, the banner offers "Begin debrief". This is the one
reflective conversation that looks across the whole session rather than a single
shift. Your dashboard gives you a set of wide-angle questions for it, for example:

- Looking across all eight shifts, what pattern do you see in your own decisions?
- Where did your leadership instincts serve you well, and where did they let you
  down?
- What did the hidden drivers reveal about how you were actually leading?
- Which shift felt the most different to manage, and why?
- What is one thing you will try differently on Monday morning?

Let this run. This is where individual shift moments join up into a leadership
signature people can actually take away. Watch for genuine reflection versus
polished defending, and again, mind the quiet.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
11. CLOSING AND THE REPORT
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When the conversation is done, press "Close session". You can then use "Download
report" in the control strip to open a clean, printable session summary you can
share with the room or keep for your records. Leave the final leaderboard up for
half a minute while people settle, thank the room, and point them to any
follow-up material.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
12. WHEN THINGS WOBBLE (TROUBLESHOOTING)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

- A team's laptop drops out. The game reconnects automatically when the wifi
  recovers, and they rejoin the same team without losing their place. If it hangs,
  they can refresh the page; their team is remembered on that laptop.
- You lose your dashboard. Reopen it in the same browser you created it in and it
  should let you straight back in. This is why you keep that tab open and do not
  hand your link around.
- A team cannot join. Check they are typing the exact five-character code, in
  capitals, and that the session is still open. Codes are case-insensitive on
  entry but must match.
- Nobody can do anything and the codes stop working. That usually means the server
  has restarted and the session is gone. There is no recovery; you start a fresh
  session. This is why nobody should deploy or restart anything during a live run.
- Do not point security scanners at the live site during a session. If you are
  doing any technical testing, that is a separate, offline exercise.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
13. QUICK REFERENCE
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Teams per session          2 to 8 (4 is ideal)
  Session code               5 characters, you share it
  Shifts                     8
  Shift length               5 minutes (ends early if all teams submit)
  Briefing                   11 guided steps, you set the pace
  Disruption chance          50 percent per shift, independent each shift
  Disruption timing          somewhere in the first half of the shift
  Disruption fairness        same moment for every team; you cannot trigger it
  Expected disruptions       about 4 across a full session, but it varies
  Live issues                always 3 per shift
  Alerts                     up to 2, plus 1 more during a disruption
  Decisions                  7 inputs across 5 tabs
  Confidence                 Cautious ~0.75, Measured 1.00, Confident ~1.35
  Hidden drivers             trust, capability, safety risk, leadership consistency
  Baseline history           16 weeks per team

  Your two companion documents:
    debrief-questions.md          the full open-question bank, by theme
    handover-facilitator-crib.md  the answer key to Ray's unreliable handover

Run it warm, keep out of the shifts, and do your best work in the debriefs. The
game will always hand you a way in.
